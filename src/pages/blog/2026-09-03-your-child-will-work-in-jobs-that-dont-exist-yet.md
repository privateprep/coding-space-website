When the internet went mainstream in the late 1990s, most people thought it would change retail. It did, but it also created entire categories of jobs nobody was training for: web developers, SEO specialists, UX designers, digital marketers. By 2004, when Facebook launched out of a Harvard dorm room, "social media manager" wasn't a job title anywhere. Five years later, companies had whole departments for it.

![Steve Jobs, Co-Founder of Apple, 1984](/img/future-of-work-pic-6.jpeg "Steve Jobs, Co-Founder of Apple, 1984")

###### Steve Jobs, Co-Founder of Apple, 1984

The iPhone came out in 2007. Within three years, the App Store had created an economy around mobile software, and a new profession, the app developer, that hadn't meaningfully existed before. Kids who had learned to code in high school were suddenly in enormous demand for a skill that wasn't on anyone's radar when they started learning it.

By the early 2010s, companies were sitting on more data than they knew what to do with, and the term "data scientist" started appearing in job postings around 2012. A decade earlier, most of those people would have been statisticians or analysts; the role as we know it didn't exist.

![Bill Gates, Co-Founder of Microsoft, 1983](/img/tcs-future-of-work-pic3.jpg "Bill Gates, Co-Founder of Microsoft, 1983")

###### Bill Gates, Co-Founder of Microsoft, 1983

The rise of platforms like Uber, Airbnb, and Amazon's third-party marketplace brought another wave: trust and safety specialists, dynamic pricing analysts, platform operations roles built entirely around managing digital ecosystems at scale. Then came deep learning going mainstream, and with it, machine learning engineers, AI researchers, and eventually a whole field around AI ethics and algorithmic auditing that nobody was studying in graduate school until recently.

![Demis Hassabis (Co-Founder)  & David Silver (Lead Researcher) DeepMind > Google DeepMind, 1998](/img/tcs-future-of-work-pic-2.webp "Demis Hassabis (Co-Founder)  & David Silver (Lead Researcher) DeepMind > Google DeepMind, 1998")

###### Demis Hassabis (Co-Founder)  & David Silver (Lead Researcher) DeepMind > Google DeepMind, 1998

Today people get paid to train AI models, write the prompts that power customer-facing tools, review outputs for bias, and build agents that do work autonomously. In 2020, none of those were real job categories. Some of them didn't have names yet.

This isn't meant to be alarming. It's just the context worth sitting with when you're thinking about what skills actually matter for kids growing up right now.

The honest answer is that no one knows exactly which specific jobs will define the job market in 2035 or 2040. Anyone who tells you otherwise is guessing. What we can say with more confidence is that the skills underneath those jobs tend to be consistent, even when the tools and titles change completely.

![Melanie Perkins & Cliff Obrecht   Co-founders of Canva, 2007](/img/tcs-future-of-work-pic-5.jpg)

###### Melanie Perkins & Cliff Obrecht, Co-founders of Canva, 2007 

Being able to pick up something unfamiliar and figure it out. Working through a problem when you don't have all the information you need. Building something from nothing, which means tolerating the gap between "I have an idea" and "I have something that works." These hold. They've held through every wave of technological change so far.

Coding teaches all of them, but not automatically. It depends a lot on how it's taught. A student who spends a year following step-by-step tutorials comes away knowing how to replicate things. A student who spends a year staring at a bug they introduced themselves, asking why it's happening, and figuring out how to fix it comes away knowing how to think.

At The Coding Space, we're deliberate about which one we're building. Instructors use the Socratic Method, meaning they ask questions rather than hand over answers. A student who's stuck on why their code isn't working won't hear "add this line here." They'll hear "what do you think is happening on line 12?" That's frustrating sometimes, especially early on. It's also exactly what makes the skill transferable later.

![Dario Amodei and Daniela Amodei, Co-Founders Anthropic, 2023](/img/future-of-work7.png "Dario Amodei and Daniela Amodei, Co-Founders Anthropic, 2023")

###### Dario Amodei and Daniela Amodei, Co-Founders of Anthropic, 2023

Small class sizes matter here too. At a 4:1 ratio, there's no way to coast. Students are actually building things, not watching someone else build them. The projects are their own ideas: games, animations, tools, websites, which means they care about getting them to work. And caring about getting something to work is where most of the real learning happens.

No one can future-proof a kid's career. But you can give them the kind of practice that holds up when the tools change again, because they will. They always do.
